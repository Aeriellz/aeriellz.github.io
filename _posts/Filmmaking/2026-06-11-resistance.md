I have bitched about randomly being unable to write and being miserable about it before. More than once—that's just writing. I make a point to only publicly cry about it when something is different, and it is different this time. Considerably so. This time, the annual walk of shame of complain about the thing that keeps me alive comes with unexpected joy of doing so.

While before I was conscious of the reasons while I felt miserable, but was [unable to solve the problem](https://aeriellz.github.io/filmmaking/2025/09/13/the-secret.html), last month I was hit with something I hadn't even *thought* about in years: writer's block.

I don't even believe in writer's block. What gives? 

What gives is that when I start working on a script, get a good chunk of the structure down, am feeling really good about it, and everything comes to a halt like always—well, usually I'm doing silly things with friends: short films where I can push through with their help or just ignore the problem and shoot anyway, because nothing matters and I'll be too high on sleep deprivation for that weekend anyway to notice the glaring issues.

This time there was nothing I could do but look at them. This time I was alone, and writing a feature length that you're taking seriously is nothing like a dumb short you're gonna shoot in a weekend with no budget and a random camera. This is something I'm writing for big budgets, on spec, that needs to function better than just well enough if anyone is gonna pay the amount of money needed to make it. It's deeply character-driven, expensive, and it's a story that matters to me, something I care about deeply. A beast more complex and intimidating than usual, and there's *nothing* beyond it than just writing it. Nothing but a painfully clear viewport of my own limits.

So one day I sat down to write after avoiding it for two weeks and... I couldn't. I knew I wouldn't be able to, and I was avoiding it for that exact reason. 

That should've been my first clue, and the fact that started crying should've been the second. I do mean *crying*, actual ugly-wailing-blubbering-river-of-tears levels of crying. This was no dainty moment, this was rage and despair in ways that I now think of as both shameful and cathartic. No one was *dying*, but it doesn't feel nice when the pillar holding your entire personality suddenly goes missing. I sat down, put the computer in front of me, a few sheets of paper because I'm from the 90s, and stared.

There's scenes I know need to be figured out, I know what should happen, but it doesn't. I'm paralized, uncreative. There's no pushing it, because there's nothing to push *from*. Effort needs something to grip against, it needs attrition, of some kind, a place to start. This felt a lot like being dead on the inside, a vacum. Once things poured out of me so easily, and now my soul was barren soil, dried and cracked. Unfit for harvest.

I knew that over the years of studying and learning and reading and crafting and trying to become something someone said was ideal I had lost something. I'm a structure writer, nowadays. I believe in understanding where the story is going, why it's going there; I believe in everything I've learned, and in many ways I need the security of that scaffolding under raw creativity, while at the same time understanding that it is not, and cannot, be the only thing holding a story together.

The problem I was facing was that of not even knowing what I lost. While as a teen I could write things that made absolutely no sense freely and have stupid fun at the moment, that fun was gone. Back then I'd get frustrated with my writing because it didn't feel like a *story*, but now I had the opposite problem. It felt all very professional, but it was miserable doing it, and it had no humanity.

When you work too hard on understanding, a piece of your gets buried under logic. *Human* is not efficient, and it's not logical, and it's not predictable. What was it that would fill this story with humanity, when conscious designing it took that humanity away from it?

How do you find out what's missing when you know that is *is* missing, but you don't know what it is?

I could only think *why won't she talk to me?* and I barely know who *she* was. This character, this non-person, why can't I know where this carefully crafted shadow is going? These words don't feel real, and they don't fit, and nothing works, it doesn't slide together the way it used to—when did it stop? How did it stop, when now I know what it needs, how to perfectly st the pieces together, what is missing here, what—

*Why won't she talk to me?*

Who's *she*?

I've been in contact with new writers again, rather recently. People who just started, who haven't been slogged by "having to" anything, and who are still so full of everything that I lost. One of them became a friend. She's older than me, but in this she's so young. Blessed with that beginner mind, with the glinting eyes of not knowing. Everything is a discovery to her, and she's always sending me writer memes, jokes about how your characters are always disobeying, doing their own thing. Going places you never wanted them to go and messing everything up. It sounded so naive, for a while, but that day I thought *why won't she talk to me* and I realized—the character.

I saw an idea on the web: if she won't talk to you, maybe you can reach out? 

Resistance. Reach out to a *character*, the absurd! What are they, a *person?* They're not real. I am you God, and you will obey me, live out your destiny and become whatever it is that I tell you to be.

A good chunk of the story is set in France, and so I wrote in french.

> *Tu es là depuis longtemps, mais tu ne m'as jamais leissé entre. Porquoi?* 
> 
> *Je ne peux pas voir ton visage, je ne peux pas voir ce que tu as fait, ce que tu ferais. Et pourtant je te connais si bien, dans les points communs que tu as avec moi. Ils ne sont pas nombreux, mais il sont suffisants.*
> 
> *Je sais que tu te sens seule. Que tu es vraiment putain de furieuse. Contre tout. Ton sœur. Contre vous-meme. Leon. Maman. Une colère qui n'a où aller. Toujours calm. Ici c'est un endroit genial pour être enragé. Vous ne pouvez rien y faire. Sourire aux caméras!* 
> 
> *Tu veux que les choses fonctionnent, mais quand elles fonctionnent, tu n'y prends aucun plaisir. Tu passes à autre chose, puis à une encore, sans jamais èchouer, et pourtant tu n'est jamais heureux.*
> 
> *Au final, il est tout simplement trop difficile d'écrire sur un problème qu'on ne sait pas résoudre.*

The moment I recognized her solitude, the dam broke. Cue the waterworks. Do I speak French? Barely. But it came through brokenly, raw, so fittingly awful. A character I forced to be lively and loud and reckless was none of that, really. She was quiet, deliberate, and read to crack open with the things she won't let out.

Why won't she talk to me? Why won't you disobey, why won't you fight against the designer of your world and forge your own path!

But had *I* done it? I've been obeying and doing what needs to be done for so long. The very idea of *a character that won't talk to me* sounded so silly; my first reaction to myself was shame. Shame that I was still thinking so whimsically, so magically, but *christ*, isn't that what I so much advocate for?

Good god, was that what I had lost—this most precious thing to me, this instinct of play?

A while back I talked about taking myself too seriously, and here was the antidote, all along, the simple childlike wonder of fantasy. Of being so profoundly human, of believing in the people that talk to me inside of my head. Letting myself dream—*oh, this would be the perfect actor to cast for this*. Imagine her in this dress! I think that's her favorite ice cream. Giving life to my teddy bear, knowing what he likes, why he's sad. His favorite songs.

At 15 I was depressed, miserable, and I wrote little stories instead of paying attention to math classes. The worlds inside of my head where solace, a rejection of real life. That teen, that young girl I was, was wrong in so many ways that I've wholeheartedly hated her ever since. She was selfish, arrogant, aloof. I never thought I'd find anything she'd done well. I never thought one day she'd teach me something, again, but in so many ways she was a child and as one she knew things I'd forget and need back in 15 more years. The time I spent away from her was the time she spent holding onto an openness that was both excessive and imperative. Her mind didn't reject anything, it had no resistance. Things flowed freely, good or bad. Everything was accepted, wether it was the concept of a character telling her of their day, or her own voice, telling her she wasn't good enough.

I forgave her for not knowing how to limit herself, but never thought something of what she did had any value. Now, in order to go forwards, I find myself going back. I open doors I closed for self preservation and undo resistance, again. With a lot more control and care, which came with growing up. A dose of fear.

I found pictures of actors and directors and of people and they were inspiring, again. It was exciting, I felt so light, so alive. So much of the story clicked into place right then and there, talking to family and friends and to myself, and *to her*. *I was talking to her*. I was talking to my characters, and they were answering and they were mad at me for pushing them out for so so so long and I couldn't stop crying.

Now, a few weeks later, everything still feels a little bit fragile. Being an adult doesn't stop, and it's hard to know how to be wide open to yourself like that. I have to relearn some strategies, create others. And still, I feel freer. I feel like me.

I was resisting whimsy, and fun, and unmotivated delight. Something we associate with youth for being so natural to the young, but that it has no age, truly. We are all creatures of play, I know that for a fact. It's high time I'm reminded to stop so needlessly opposing it.
